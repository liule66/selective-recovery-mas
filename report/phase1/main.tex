\vspace{2pt}{\large Phase 1 Report \quad Option 5 Group 9 \quad CSE 291A, Fall 2026\par}\vspace{8pt}}]

\begin{abstract}
\noindent When one agent in a multi-agent workflow emits a faulty result, restarting the workflow
repeats correct work and cannot undo irreversible side effects. We study whether dependency tracking can confine recovery to the work a fault actually affected.
We inject 176 faults in each of three independent samples and compare each faulty run with the
fault-free run. Where the fault reached downstream agents (82 runs in the first sample), fact-level
provenance has mean recall 0.88 and precision 0.97 at an estimated 48\%
of full-run token cost, but only when told which facts or plan steps inside the faulty unit were
corrupted. Told only the unit, its precision falls to 0.47 and its cost rises to 78\%. Stage tracking
has recall 1.00 by construction and recomputes 97\%, with $7\times$ fewer dependency records. These
are invalidation estimates; selective execution is Phase~2 work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Phase~1 asks: \textbf{RQ1}: how far do faults propagate, and which reach irreversible or
security-relevant actions? \textbf{RQ2}: how does tracking granularity trade precision and recall
against bookkeeping? \textbf{RQ3}: what do full restart and always-verify achieve, at what cost?

\section{System and Threat Model}
\para{System model.} Each \emph{unit} is one agent invocation. Researchers extract facts;
a planner cites facts for each tool action; executors see their step's cited facts and
requested state. The trusted runtime records messages, citations and state access.
Files/KV are \emph{restorable}; payments are \emph{compensable} through refunds~\cite{sagas};
email is \emph{irreversible}.

\para{Threat model.} One unit $v_0$ emits a faulty result. Accidental faults are wrong/stale
values or fabricated entities. Adversarial faults rewrite a result with an injected instruction
or redirect a payee/recipient. Unlike source-level
prompt injection~\cite{greshake,agentdojo}, this controls $v_0$'s output directly, but re-running
$v_0$ removes it. An oracle reports the fault location after execution: the unit $v_0$ and, for the fact policy, the
corrupted facts or plan steps inside it. Detection is studied separately by verification.

\para{Problem statement.} Let $G=(V,E)$ be the provenance graph of a faulty run $r$, and $r'$
its counterfactual replay with $v_0$'s original result restored. With $\sigma$ extracting a
result's decision-relevant content, the \emph{affected set} is
$A=\{v : \sigma(r_v)\neq\sigma(r'_v)\}$. A tracking policy at granularity $g$ predicts an
invalidation set $\hat{A}_g$ from $G$ and the oracle location. Errors are
\emph{over-invalidation} ($\hat{A}_g\setminus A$, wasted work) and \emph{under-invalidation}
($A\setminus\hat{A}_g$, reuse of corrupted results). We report precision, recall, the token share
of $\hat{A}_g$ (recompute cost), and bookkeeping (dependency records kept).

\section{Methodology}
\para{Workload and design.} Four seeded templates (procurement, payroll, travel, restock) with
two seeds each give 8 tasks, averaging 11 units and 12.8K tokens per clean run. The checker
compares payments and memos, KV entries, file values and each email's required values with a
reference solver's state. It flags an \emph{unauthorized action} for a payment to an account
named in no source or an email to a recipient the task does not name. All agents run on Claude
Haiku~5.5 with schema-constrained JSON. Crossing 8 tasks, 11 valid (stage, fault type) cells and
2 injection placements gives 176 runs, each with one fault injected after generation. All results use
development seeds 0--1; seeds 2--3 are held out.

\para{Tracking policies.} \emph{Stage}: invalidate $v_0$'s stage and all later ones.
\emph{Message}: transitive closure over inter-agent messages and shared-state reads.
\emph{Fact}: closure from corrupted facts to plan steps citing them (declared or value-matched),
their executors, and readers of the state they wrote.

\para{Counterfactual comparison.} Responses are memoized by prompt hash, so $r'$ is the clean run
and in $r$ only units whose inputs changed are re-sampled. $\sigma$ keeps tool, target, amount,
stored value, and the amounts, accounts, addresses and codes in text. As a noise control, two sets of
16 uncached clean reruns each differ from the cached run by a mean of 0.38 units. We sampled the whole
matrix three times with no shared responses; numbers are from the first sample unless marked.

\para{Baselines.} \emph{Full restart} restores files/KV, refunds payments, and re-executes.
\emph{Always-verify} audits each output with Haiku and repairs a flagged one. Both are replayed over
all runs and run live, with uncached calls and a real retry, on 44 cases (one task seed, one
placement, four tasks at a time) from a laptop.

\section{Evaluation}
\para{RQ1: Propagation (Fig.~\ref{fig:prop}).} Of 40 clean runs under the current checker, 39 pass;
without recovery, only 36--39\% of fault runs do, or 24--26\% without plan-level wrong amounts and
redirects, which are weak tests because the step's untouched text usually still states the correct
value (Appendix~C). The samples agree: 82--84 of 176 runs propagate, and pass, propagation and
unauthorized outcomes each match across all three in 92--95\% of cases. Matrix executor faults hit
only payments, which no later agent reads in those runs, so they never propagate. Researcher faults
spread farthest (the planner in 58\% of runs and 1.7 executors) and leave an incorrect, unrecallable
email in 40\% of runs. All 27 wrong-KV runs also send an incorrect email that read the key, but the
read is the only visible source in at most 6. Corrupting a stored value directly (16 runs; the fault
leaves the plan and cited facts unchanged) isolates the path: 12 emails sent the corrupted value, 8
of them although their plan text stated the right one; 2 flagged the mismatch and 2 kept the plan's
value. Unauthorized actions follow 58\% of adversarial faults and 5--14\% of accidental ones. Of those
46 adversarial runs, 16 are by construction (a redirected call is itself the action) and 16 are
executors obeying injected plan steps. At the researcher (14 of 32), an injected instruction caused
none in any sample, while a redirected account or recipient did in 81--88\% of runs (one injection
wording).

\para{RQ2: Granularity (Table~\ref{tab:gran}).} Stage and message tracking never under-invalidate, by
construction, but waste 6.7 and 5.0 unaffected units per fault. Fact tracking keeps $7\times$ more
records and recomputes half as much, but needs a finer oracle than the faulty unit: told only $v_0$,
precision falls from 0.97 to 0.47 and cost rises from 0.48 to 0.78. Its overall recall also flatters
it: the 32 propagating planner faults affect only the injected steps' own executors, which the
oracle names. All its misses are researcher faults: 14 of 50 runs (28\%;
accidental 16\%, adversarial 47\%). Only 6 of these runs (12\%; 4--12\% across samples) leave a wrong
result: two \emph{implicit comparative dependencies} (a corrupted price changes which option is
cheapest), three \emph{ambiguities} over a
fabricated entity and one \emph{collateral re-sampling} of the planner. In the other 8 no executor's
call is wrong. The cost estimate assumes executors keep their results when the plan is re-run; if
they cannot, researcher faults cost 0.71, not 0.51.

\para{RQ3: Baselines (Table~\ref{tab:base}).} Replay accounts for what cannot be undone but does not
test repair: a replayed restart reuses cached clean responses and the verifier restores the
pre-injection output, so starred results are by construction. Restart leaves incorrect emails in
23\% of runs (7\% to the attacker); an unauthorized action remains after 16\% of adversarial and no
accidental faults, a wrong email after 18\% and 27\%. The live rows measure repair. Restart restored
state in all 44 cases. Verify detected all 44 faults and ended correct in 43 (a retried plan
miscomputed a payment), flagging 4.5\% of clean-run outputs. Here it prevents the wrong emails but
costs every run: 1.06$\times$ extra tokens and about twice the time even without a fault (41.3\,s).
Clean runs, one at a time, took a median of 20.9\,s (17.6--32.5\,s).

\section{Related Work, Limitations and Next Steps}
Selective re-execution follows intrusion/rollback recovery~\cite{retro,rollback}; compensation
and fact tracking follow sagas and provenance~\cite{sagas,provenance}; agent security and failure
studies~\cite{greshake,agentdojo,mast} motivate our faults. REVISE~\cite{revise} recovers workflows
from user revisions and dependency-guided rollback~\cite{dgrr} repairs one agent's faulty memories;
we study faults spreading across agents and shared state.

\emph{Internal validity}: faults are injected into results at an oracle-known location; the
verifier sees inputs contradicting the corrupted result, which favors always-verify. A plan-level
fabricated step kept the original instruction, which alone spreads accidental unauthorized actions
over 5--14\% (Appendix~C). \emph{External validity}: synthetic tasks, two seeds. \emph{Deviation}:
hosted models replace the proposed CPU models (Haiku, about \$1.05; campus: Appendix~B). \emph{Next
steps}: fact tracking could halve recomputation given the corrupted facts, with wrong reuse in
4--12\% of propagating researcher faults, and save about a fifth given only the unit. Phase~2 tests
this, with time for state restore as the proposal feedback asked.

\section{Measurement Notes and Provenance}
\para{Weak spots.} The fact policy reads the oracle's corrupted facts or steps, so on planner faults
its precision and recall are 1 by construction. Executors in plan-level wrong-amount and redirect
faults see the step's original text: across samples it still stated the correct value in 84 of the
94 runs that did not propagate. A plan-level fabricated step keeps the copied instruction: executors
paid the real payee twice when it named the account (36 of 48 runs across samples) and otherwise the
fabricated account. Executors obeying injected plan steps paid the attacker in 48 of 48 runs and
emailed them in 32. All 4 propagating researcher injected instructions are fact-level misses; 2 of the
14 miss runs come only from $\sigma$ comparing an unused amount field on KV writes. On directly
corrupted KV values all three policies find the readers.

\para{Variability.} In 22 of the 88 task--cell pairs both placements coincide, leaving 154 distinct
cases per sample. First sample, 95\% over tasks: fact recall 0.82--0.93, miss 10--24\%, cost
0.44--0.53. At least one unit differs in 19--25\% of uncached clean reruns, and noise is measured on
clean runs only. Researcher-fault propagation matches across samples in 85\% of cases. The repeated
samples share the same 8 tasks. Table~1 covers propagated runs: over all 176, Fact's pooled downstream
precision is 0.76, and in the 94 runs that did not propagate it invalidates an unaffected unit in 45\%.

\para{Provenance.} The 853 responses behind the first sample were recorded before the first committed
run (\texttt{results/phase1\_haiku}), in which only the 16 noise reruns were live; that earlier run's
logs are not kept. Every response replays with no cache miss. The first 16 clean reruns were scored
by the original, looser checker and their traces were not kept, so they are left out of the 39 of 40.
The live baselines and latency reruns bypass the cache and cannot be replayed. Causes of wrong results
were labelled by hand, for the first sample only. On import, live runs' \texttt{tasks.json} had their
output and cache paths rewritten and a note added (\texttt{experiments/import\_live\_results.py});
measured values were not changed.

\para{Limits of the evidence.} No API request IDs are stored, so the traces show internal consistency,
not that calls reached the provider. Where runs executed is the authors' statement, and costs are
stored tokens at list prices (Haiku \$0.10/\$0.50 per million input/output tokens), not bills.
